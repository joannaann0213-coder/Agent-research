## 1. History: who banned or restricted ChatGPT-type tools in 2023, and what did they do afterwards?

### Takeaway
The 2023 restrictions were real but were aimed at the consumer ChatGPT chat assistant, were mostly imposed through generic third-party-software controls, were framed as "until vetted", and are known mainly from memos and anonymous sources. By November 2026 the best-documented cases have moved to approved enterprise deployments (Samsung in June 2026, Goldman Sachs engineers, Accenture), with residual limits by business unit, job role or budget; I found no post-2023 evidence either way for Verizon, Northrop Grumman or Bank of America.

### Inferences
- The 2023 actions were default-deny reactions applied through existing third-party-software and data-handling controls to a consumer chat product; they were not AI-coding-tool policies in the 2026 sense. Only Samsung's ban is tied to a documented source-code leak.
- The best-documented reversal (Samsung) took about three years and kept a tighter regime for the chip division, which suggests risk-tiering by business unit instead of a single company-wide rule.
- For the firms where I have 2026 evidence (Samsung, Goldman, Accenture), "ban" has been replaced by "approved enterprise deployment plus scoping" (who may use it, with which data, at what cost).

## 2. Statistics: how common are bans, shadow use, formal policies, and which tools dominate (2023 to 2026)?

### Takeaway
No single survey tracks ban rates on a like-for-like basis from 2023 to 2026, but every data point I found points the same way: outright prohibition fell from a headline majority "considering or implementing" bans in 2023 to a small minority by 2024 to 2026, while unapproved ("shadow") use remains large and formal governance lags adoption. Most figures on bans and leakage come from security vendors and are interested-party data.

### Inferences
- In the cases documented here, restrictions in 2026 are rarely justified by the generic 2023 fear ("our data will train their model"). They are justified by (i) who the vendor is (a competitor, or a vendor in a political dispute), (ii) what the agent can do (execute commands, hold credentials), and (iii) what it costs.
- The competitor and dogfooding motive and the security motive are hard to separate in the Alibaba case: the stated reason was a "back-door" risk, the replacement was the company's own product, and the two companies were already in a public dispute. Treat the stated reason as the company's position, not as an established technical finding.

I found no evidence on these in this session, which reflects the missing searches, not the absence of cases.

## 6. Alternatives to a ban: how enterprises make external tools acceptable

### Takeaway
The mainstream alternative is a managed deployment: an enterprise contract with the vendor, centrally enforced client configuration, optional routing through the company's own cloud account or an internal gateway, and scoping by role, repository and budget. I verified the configuration mechanisms in vendor documentation titles and summaries and several named deployments, but did not verify contract terms such as zero data retention.

## 8. Does banning work?

### Takeaway
The available survey and telemetry evidence, mostly from interested security vendors, says prohibition does not stop use: tools are used at organisations that prohibit them, and a third or more of users go through personal accounts. The strongest behavioural evidence that managed adoption works better is Netskope's fall in personal-account use as sanctioned tools spread, and Samsung's replacement of a three-year ban with an enterprise deployment. I did not search productivity or recruiting evidence.

### Cited Findings
- [A, IP] Checkmarx, July 2024: 15% of organisations prohibit AI code-generation tools, yet 99% of respondents say such tools are in use. — [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/15-of-companies-ban-code-ai-but-99-of-developers-use-it-anyway/)
- [A, IP] Cisco, January 2024: 48% admit entering non-public company information into generative-AI tools even as 27% of organisations had banned them at least temporarily. — [Cisco newsroom](https://newsroom.cisco.com/c/r/newsroom/en/us/a/y2024/m01/organizations-ban-use-of-generative-ai-over-data-privacy-security-cisco-study.html)

### Inferences
- The figures showing that bans "do not work" come from vendors that sell monitoring and governance products, measured on their own customers or on self-selected survey panels; they are consistent with one another, but none is neutral. - A ban appears enforceable where the company controls the network, the device and the software list and offers an in-house substitute (Alibaba, ByteDance); it appears leaky where staff have no sanctioned alternative (2023 to 2024 data).

## 9. Direction of travel in late 2026

### Takeaway
The trend is a split, not a swing: general-purpose bans have faded and adoption is near-universal under approved-tool regimes, while new restrictions in 2026 are targeted by vendor (competitors and geopolitics), by role, by cost and by blast radius. The only new outright bans I found in 2026 came from a company with its own competing tool (Alibaba) and from the US government acting against a single vendor; ByteDance tightened restrictions without a reported full ban.

### Cited Findings
- [A] Near-universal adoption: 83% of developers use AI at work (Stack Overflow, October 2026); 90% of technology professionals (DORA, 2025); Gartner forecasts 90% of enterprise software engineers on AI code assistants by 2028. — [Stack Overflow](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2026/ai); [DORA](https://dora.dev/dora-report-2025/); [Gartner, 1 July 2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-07-01-gartner-identifies-the-top-strategic-trends-in-software-engineering-for-2025-and-beyond)

### Inferences
- For the question "do big internet companies ban tools like Claude Code and Codex and allow only in-house tools?", the cross-industry evidence gathered here supports a conditional answer: outside companies that sell competing tools or sit inside a geopolitical dispute, the 2026 norm is approved external tools under enterprise controls; an in-house-only rule is documented in this file where the employer has its own rival tool (Alibaba); ByteDance and the US hyperscalers are covered by other researchers.
- Restrictions are shifting from "may we use it?" to "who, on what, through which control plane, at what cost?". Cost caps and role scoping are the fastest-growing forms of restriction in the 2026 reporting I found.
